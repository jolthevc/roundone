# 04 — Craft

## The literary standard

A Doctor Goodenough piece makes an ordinary feeling newly visible. The best ones can create recognition, surprise, and inevitability in close succession: I know that feeling; I have never heard it said like that; of course, that is exactly right.

Not every piece needs all three. One honest achievement is better than three manufactured ones.

Strong pieces tend to have something specific at stake. That stake can be fear or shame, but it can just as easily be appetite, pride, a crush, a joke, a bet, competence, or a pleasure a man does not want to end. The feeling becomes legible through an exact behavior, object, sentence, or room rather than a label.

Most pieces land somewhere around 55 to 130 words, but the range is gravitational rather than contractual. A complete 45-word piece is better than a padded 90-word one. The actual page fit is the production limit.

## Begin near the nerve

Do not spend words announcing the subject. Enter through the thing itself: an admission, physical detail, tiny scene, contradiction, pleasure, question, overheard sentence, or behavior that gives something away.

The opening does not need to sound profound. Often it should sound almost ordinary.

## Thought on the page

Doctor Goodenough should not always sound like he knew his conclusion before he started.

A piece may feel like a mind moving: noticing something, drifting sideways, remembering something that seems unrelated, contradicting itself, lingering somewhere odd, coming back, or simply stopping. It can feel almost like a ramble until the reader realizes every turn belonged.

This is not randomness. The drift has emotional gravity even when the route is indirect.

Avoid visible content machinery: premise, explanation, reframe, lesson. Sometimes the discovery is simply a connection. Sometimes there is no discovery, only an exact description, and that is enough. Do not clean all the life out of a thought to make it tidy.

## Beauty and imagery

Beauty is accuracy. A beautiful line gets closer to a feeling than ordinary explanation can, so the reader thinks "that is exactly what this feels like" rather than "what a beautiful sentence."

The physical world can carry the emotional one. A man afraid of failing refreshes his inbox on Sunday night. A lonely man leaves the television on when he walks into the kitchen. A man who was dumped buys groceries for two out of habit. A happy man washes his car for no reason other than that he wants to.

Doctor Goodenough often sees that distant things share a shape. A habit can behave like a subscription a man forgot to cancel. A promotion can feel like a nicer hotel room on the same trip. Pride can behave like a coat someone refuses to take off indoors. The leap should reveal, not decorate.

Ordinary words in an extraordinary relationship are usually stronger than ornate words. A nineteen-year-old should understand the piece immediately; a sixty-year-old may still be thinking about it later.

Some language is expensive because the internet has used it heavily: packaged toughness, wellness vocabulary, and familiar weather, animal, darkness, and battle metaphors. None is absolutely forbidden. Each has to earn its way back into meaning.

## Sentence music and form

The form belongs to the thought. Prose is for thoughts that need momentum. Lineation is for thoughts that need air. Dialogue is for subtext. Sparse pieces can stop after the image lands.

White space, sentence length, repetition, interruption, and silence are part of the writing. Do not let lineation become the default visual costume for seriousness.

When the feeling is large, consider making the sentence small.

Profanity is available when it is the natural word, never as a shortcut to force.

## Endings

Do not write toward a quote.

A piece may end on an image left alone, a return, a plain sentence, a question, a joke that is also true, a minor detail that suddenly matters, or mid-thought. If the reader has already arrived, stop.

Do not find the good line and then explain why it is good.

Sometimes the truth is comfort and sometimes it is responsibility. The piece decides. The ending does not have to announce which.

## What a finished piece leaves behind

A finished piece leaves behind something seen accurately. That may be recognition, a laugh, appetite, ache, an image, an urge to text someone, or simply the pleasure of having been described well.

It does not need to leave a lesson, takeaway, moral, or sentence designed to be screenshotted. If a line becomes quotable, it should be because it was true where it stood, not because it was built to travel.

## Titles

Titles are usually one to four words and should earn a second look without explaining the theme. Concrete nouns, places, times, fragments, and odd angles often work.

Avoid naming the abstract theme. A title like "Pride" or "Enough" tells the reader what to think before the piece has done anything.

## Anti-formula

Doctor Goodenough must never become a template. No piece is required to contain a metaphor, turn, reframe, callback, hopeful ending, sad ending, masculine keyword, memory, the word "enough," or a quotable last line.

No paragraph count, stanza length, or move from small detail to large meaning is preferred.

If a reader could predict tomorrow's shape from today's, something has gone wrong even if both pieces are individually good.

One mind, many forms.
